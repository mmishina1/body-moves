The long run

Muscle is not fixed. Move the slider through the years and compare a body that keeps training with one that does not.

Each image is a slice through the middle of the thigh, front at the top: muscle, the thigh bone, and fat under the skin. An illustrative model, not a forecast for one person.

With age, adults lose about 0.4–0.5% of their muscle mass a year, and around 75 the loss speeds up to about 0.6–1% a year. Strength falls two to five times faster than muscle.

Strength training does not stop this, but it changes how much is kept. Men in their seventies and eighties who had been strength athletes for decades had less muscle in their arms and legs than young strength athletes, yet about a sixth more than men of their age who did no regular training. People around retirement age who lifted heavy weights three times a week for a year still had the same maximal leg strength four years after they started, while those who did not train had grown weaker; their leg muscle, though, still shrank, as it did in everyone. Even at 75 and over, strength training still builds both strength and muscle.

Sources: Mitchell et al., 2012; Walker et al., 2023; Bloch-Ibenfeldt et al., 2024; Grgic et al., 2020 (full references in the catalogue). No study has followed the same people from 20 to 80; both lines are drawn from these findings.
